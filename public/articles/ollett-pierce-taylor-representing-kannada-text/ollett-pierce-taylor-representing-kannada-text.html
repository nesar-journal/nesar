1 Introduction

  • 1We are happy to acknowledge the influence of Arlo Griffiths, which this paper manifests quite obviously: both his programmatic statements about transliteration, found in the article he co-wrote with Andrea Acri (Acri and Griffiths 2014) and his own exemplary practice, especially in his epigraphic projects, with which one of the authors (Ollett) has been an occasional collaborator.

  • 2Although our primary concern in this paper is with Kannada, our remarks might apply, mutatis mutandis, to related South Indian scripts, including especially Telugu. The Kannada and Telugu scripts are very similar, having developed in parallel from the Deccani successors of the Sātavāhana Brāhmī script. See Sivaramamurty (1999) [1948]).

In this paper we present a set of conventions for representing Kannada texts from the premodern period — we focus here on literary texts and inscriptions — in print and digital media.1 By “Kannada text” we mean text in the Kannada language, which is generally written in the Kannada script.2 We will not discuss the representation of Sanskrit text written in the Kannada script, except insofar as the conventions we propose in this paper might cause conflict or confusion with conventions for representing Sanskrit text written in the Kannada script. The conventions we will discuss in this paper include the transliteration of the Kannada script into roman characters, or “romanization,” and the marking of boundaries between words, or “word separation.” The problems are related: romanization means writing the Kannada language in a script with a different logic than the Kannada script, and the separation of words with spaces has been part of that logic for several centuries now. Both of these procedures have a range of uses, from research, to teaching, to more technical applications. Scholars, to take one important use-case, very often want to present Kannada texts that are romanized and perhaps separated into words. With regard to romanization, there are a number of competing conventions, and users — including scholars, language-learners, and so on — generally use a transliteration system that accords with their needs and preferences. Similarly, a variety of approaches have been taken with respect to word-separation in transliterated Kannada text, and scholars generally find an approach that meets their immediate needs.

One test of whether a set of conventions is a “standard” is whether someone might reasonably be expected to offer an explanation for not adhering to those conventions. In this sense, none of the approaches to either transliteration or word-separation in Kannada has emerged as a “standard.” For us, a “standard” is not defined only by being in wide use, but being explicitly documented, both regarding its motivation (i.e., why decisions regarding transliteration and word separation have been taken) and its application (i.e., how it can be used in a variety of contexts). We feel that the need for standards in this area is self-evident, especially as scholarly interest in Kannada literature grows outside of Karnataka. It is our goal in this paper to present a set of conventions which, if widely adopted, can become a standard.

First, by way of introducing some of the problems that a system of transliteration should address, we will review three conditions of Kannada writing — syllabic script, scriptio continua, and sandhi. These conditions make transliteration more than a simple matter of choosing a particular roman-script character to represent a given Kannada-script character. They require a principled way of dealing with the problem of boundaries in Kannada writing: boundaries of syllables, words, and metrical units.

Then we will discuss romanization and defend our preference for a modified form of the ISO-15919 standard for transliterating Indic texts. In this section, we also discuss alternative transliteration schemes, and the application of transliteration in different contexts of use, including “strict transliteration” of inscriptions and manuscripts, and the “loose transliteration” that is generally preferred elsewhere.

We proceed to a discussion of word separation, and our suggestions for representing word boundaries both in transliterated and in Kannada-script text. Included here are recommendations for the yatibhaṅga problem: the lack of coincidence, not only between syllables and words, but between metrical boundaries and words, which raises issues because metrical units are often set on separate typographic lines.

At the beginning, however, we will offer a number of definitions in order to avoid confusion:

  • A script is a “set of conventional graphic signs designed to give visual representation to the elements of a writing system” (Wellisch 1978: 15).
  • A character is an element of the writing system that can be used independently according to the logic of that writing system. In the roman script used for English, a, b, and c are “characters” in this sense. In Indic scripts, such as Dēvanāgarī, क (ka), ङ (ṅa), and अ (a) are also “characters.”
  • A marker is an element of the writing system that cannot be used independently according to the logic of that writing system. Signs used to mark accent and vowel length are “markers,” like the macron in ā, or the acute in é. The “dependent vowel signs” in Indic scripts, for example the sign for the vowel i in कि (ki) and चि (ci), are also markers. So, too, is the virāma, the sign that cancels the inherent vowel of a character.
  • A complex character may be a character that is composed of more than one character, such as ligatures in traditional European printing, or conjunct consonants in Indic scripts, e.g., त्त (tta) = त् (t) + त (ta). It may also be a character with one or more markers, such as कि (ki) or चि (ci) above. These two kinds of “complexes” might in principle be distinguished, but we do not see a compelling reason for doing so here.
  • Transliteration is “the process of recording the graphic symbols of one writing system in terms of the corresponding graphic symbols of a second writing system” (Wellisch 1978: 31, citing the United Nations Working Group on a Single Romanization System). This term has often been contrasted with transcription, which the same group defined as “the process of recording the phonological and/or morphological elements of a language in terms of a specific writing system” (ibid.). In the scholarly context, however, “transcription” very often refers to the process of copying a manuscript, thus producing a new copy or “transcript,” which may either be in the same script, or a different script, than the manuscript from which it was copied. We will retain the scholarly usage in this document.

2 Three Conditions of Kannada Writing

Kannada texts from the premodern period were generally written under three main conditions: the use of a syllabic script, the use of scriptio continua, and the application of sandhi. The expert reader of (premodern) Kannada will have no problem reading texts that are written in this fashion. But because they are written without any word separation at all, these texts impose some difficulties on the non-expert reader. We will review each of these three conditions briefly.

Syllabic script

  • 3 Indian grammarians defined a syllable as a vowel together with any and all preceding consonants, apart from a small set of necessarily syllable-final consonants such as ṁ and ḥ. Hence kartr̥ would be syllabified as ka.rtr̥. This definition deviates somewhat from modern definitions that are based on sonority, according to which the same word would be syllabified as kar.tr̥. However, the facultative gemination that occurs before and after semivowels in Sanskrit (Aṣṭādhyāyī 8.4.46–47), which turns tatra into tattra, effectively renders both parses identical for most phonological purposes. In other words, words like tatra would also be parsed as a heavy syllable followed by a light syllable.

The Kannada script, like all Indic scripts, is syllabic. This means that the smallest unit of writing that can be used independently — the character, according to the definition above — represents a syllable. The notion of the syllable presupposed by Indic scripts is closely related to the phonological syllable, although not identical with it.3 The character for a syllable consists in either a sign for an independent vowel, or a sign for a consonant, sometimes followed by other consonant signs which are joined to it. The consonant sign is read as containing the “inherent vowel” a. The vowel associated with the consonant sign can be changed with the addition of vowel markers. The entire character — that is, the independent vowel sign, or the consonant sign along with any vowel markers — may then be followed by the sign of a dependent, syllable-final consonant. Besides vowel markers (commonly called mātrās) another important set of markers are dependent consonants (ayōgavāhas). The syllable-final nasal, or anusvāra, is the most common of these dependent consonants, and will be discussed below.

It is important to note that, while the script in general is read from left to right, this left-to-right logic does not necessarily hold at the level of the character. Certain markers might appear to the right of the consonant sign, but represent sounds that are pronounced before the consonant. Thus the “floating r” represents the sound r when it is pronounced before another consonant, or group of consonants, but it is written after the sign for that consonant:

  1. ರ್ಗ್ಗ = rgga

In other Indic scripts, markers that are pronounced after the consonant might appear to the left of the consonant sign (e.g. the marker for e and ē in the Tamil script, or i in Dēvanāgarī, or in Nandināgarī), but this situation does not arise in the Kannada script.

Scriptio continua

Scriptio continua means that the text is written without any graphic indications of word boundaries, for example, spaces between words. Generally only metrical boundaries in a verse text, or sentence boundaries in a prose text, are explicitly marked in manuscripts and inscriptions, as well as in modern printed texts. While the daṇḍa or “staff” (।) was used for all of these purposes in premodern texts, in modern printed texts, it is only used to mark metrical boundaries, as Western punctuation (periods, commas, exclamation marks, and question marks) is now generally used to mark clause and sentence boundaries.

Sandhi

  • 4Kavirājamārgaṁ (Way of the Poet-King), 2.50. Note that in our transcription we use “.” to mark boundaries between syllabic characters of the writing system, rather than the syllables that are parsed by phonological rules, although they coincide to a large degree. The rule for post-lexical syllabification is enunciated at Śabdamaṇidarpaṇaṁ (Jewel-Ornament of Language) 49 (svararahitavyañjanam avu paravarṇaman aidut’ irpuv ā saṁhiteyoḷ).

Sandhirefers to the changes to which sounds are subject because of their position vis-à-vis other sounds. Various kinds of sandhi phenomena form a constitutive feature of premodern Kannada texts, especially verse texts: if the sandhi is “undone,” the metrical pattern is destroyed. Thus, while the use of the Kannada script and scriptio continua can be thought of as orthographic choices, which pertain to norms of writing, the application of sandhi is an integral part of the linguistic and metrical structure of the texts themselves. The two major sandhi phenomena that must be considered in this connection are postlexical syllabification and hiatus avoidance. The former refers to the fact that the domain of syllabification is larger than the word. In metrical texts, it is the verse; in prose texts, it is the sentence. Hence, when vowels and consonants are parsed into syllables, boundaries between words are irrelevant. As a consequence, whenever one word ends in a consonant, and the following word begins with a vowel, these two adjacent sounds are parsed into a single syllable:4

  1. aṟipal ārpar ār → a.ṟi.pa.lā.rpa.rār → ಅ.ಱಿ.ಪ.ಲಾ.ರ್ಪ.ರಾರ್

In the Kannada script, a word-final consonant and a word-initial vowel tend to be written with a single syllabic character. This is observed relatively strictly in versified text, where the metrical pattern often requires a particular configuration of syllables, each with a specified weight, but it is observed in prose as well.

Syllabification across word boundaries is even more evident, and more important, in the case of word-final nasal consonants. In premodern Kannada, a nasal consonant that stands at the end of a syllable — and thus at the end of a word in its isolated form, before the application of sandhi — is typically written with an anusvāra, a dependent consonant sign that represents a nasal unspecified as to place of articulation. It is likely, though not directly relevant to the issue at hand, that this orthographic convention represents a phonological fact, namely, the loss of place contrasts among syllable-final nasals.

  1. vikalpam → vikalpaṁ → ವಿಕಲ್ಪಂ
  2. arasan → arasaṁ → ಅರಸಂ

When such words are followed by a word beginning with a vowel, the word-final nasal no longer stands at the end of a syllable, and the “underlying” nasal consonant, specified for place of articulation, reappears:

  1. vikalpaṁ + akkuṁ → vi.ka.lpa.ma.kkuṁ → ವಿಕಲ್ಪಮಕ್ಕುಂ
  2. arasaṁ + akkuṁ → a.ra.sa.na.kkuṁ → ಅರಸನಕ್ಕುಂ

  • 5 Nāgavarma, Chandōmbudhi (Ocean of Meters), ed. Ramaswamy Iyengar, 1.51 (p.12).

Even under such conditions of syllabification across word boundaries, a word boundary can be represented by a space in a romanized text, as the examples above show. In a Kannada-script text, however, this is not possible because of the combination of the consonant and vowel into a single grapheme. In some cases, editors have nevertheless attempted to represent the word boundary by printing a space before the word-final consonant:5

  1. śāntapūrvam akkuṁ → śān.ta.pū.rva. ma.kkuṁ → ಶಾಂತಪೂರ್ವ ಮಕ್ಕುಂ

In Epigraphia Indica, the standard practice — for Kannada as well as other languages — was to represent a word boundary within a written syllable with =. Hence Epigraphia Indica would write vikalpam=akkuṁ, arasan=akkuṁ, and śāntapūrvam=akkuṁ.

Orthographic practices such as this reflect an inherent conflict between the imperative to separate words for the sake of legibility, on the one hand, and to present a text that has been syllabified across word boundaries and written in a syllabic script, on the other.

  • 7Kavirājamārgaṁ 2.57, 2.73, and 2.94.

In addition to postlexical syllabification, another basic principle of sandhi observed in many Kannada texts is hiatus avoidance. This means that two vowels, constituting two separate syllables, are not allowed to come into contact with each other. In one set of contexts, hiatus is avoided through elision of the left-hand vowel, or lōpasandhi. The Jewel-Ornament of Language says that this strategy is followed “as long as elision does not make the meaning uncertain.”6 More specifically, however, this strategy generally targets the final vowels -u and -a, and sometimes -e. Hence:7

  1. tōrpudu adu → tō.rpu.da.du → ತೋರ್ಪುದದು
  2. pēḻvoḍe adu → pē.ḻvo.ḍa.du → ಪೇೞ್ವೊಡದು
  3. pēḻva antu → pē.ḻvan.tu → ಪೇೞ್ವಂತು

The practice of Epigraphia Indica was to mark such boundaries with the sign =, as above. Hence tōrpud=adu, pēḻvoḍ=adu, and pēḻv=antu. Some scholars choose to simply leave a space in place of the elided vowel, hence tōrpud adu, pēḻvoḍ adu, and pēḻv antu.

  • 8Kavirājamārgaṁ 1.8, 2.41, and 2.23.

In another set of contexts — generally when the left-hand vowel is e/ē or o/ō — hiatus is avoided through the insertion of a glide homorganic with the left-hand vowel, or āgamasandhi. Hence:8

  1. samavr̥tti appavōl → sa.ma.vr̥.tti.ya.ppa.vōl → ಸಮವೃತ್ತಿಯಪ್ಪವೋಲ್
  2. eṇe akkaraṁ → e.ṇe.ya.kka.raṁ → ಎಣೆಯಕ್ಕರಂ
  3. pala eḍegaḷoḷ → pa.la.ve.ḍe.ga.ḷoḷ → ಪಲವೆಡೆಗಳೊಳ್

Epigraphia Indica wrote the glide at the beginning of the following word, hence samavr̥tti yappavōl, eṇe yakkaraṁ, and pala veḍegaḷoḷ.

Both types of sandhi processes result in the non-coincidence of word and syllable boundaries. In the case of lōpasandhi, this is because the onset of the final syllable of the first word is syllabified with the nucleus of the first syllable of the following word. In the case of āgamasandhi, the quality of the final vowel of the first word determines the quality (y or v) of the consonantal glide that appears as the onset of the first syllable of the following word.

In the transition to print and digital media, the use of scriptio continua was abandoned entirely. In modern Kannada, spaces are typically printed in between words, apart from a group of “function words” (like āda or āgi) that act as grammatical suffixes. This is possible in part because of the fact that, in modern Kannada, a facultative final vowel, -u, has been added, both in writing and in pronunciation, in most of the situations where a word would otherwise have ended in a consonant.

3 Romanization

Applications

  • 9Mirror of Literature 2.178 (p. 121); Mādhava’s Ornament 2.181 (p. 64).

Romanization’s most obvious application lies in making Kannada texts legible for those who do not easily read the Kannada script. Although to some it may sound counterintuitive that people without a reading knowledge of Kannada might nevertheless want to “read” Kannada texts, there are two main groups of people who are likely to find themselves in this situation. The first includes beginning students of Kannada, for whom the nature of Kannada writing itself often poses a considerable challenge in recognizing words and thus understanding a text. (This includes even Kannada speakers who have not been trained to read the older language.) The second includes scholars who may already be proficient in another South Asian language, such as Sanskrit, and who are interested in the use of vocabulary items, themes, motifs, or ideas across South Asian languages. As an example, take the two verses below, the first being the definition of the ornament of “distinction” (vyatirēkaḥ) from Daṇḍin’s Mirror of Literature (Kāvyādarśaḥ), in Sanskrit, and the second being the corresponding definition from Mādhava’s Ornament (Mādhavālaṅkāraṁ), in Kannada:9

  1. śabdōpāttē pratītē vā sādr̥śyē vastunōr dvayōḥtatra yad bhēdakathanaṁ vyatirēkaḥ sa kathyatē
  2. viditaṁ vastugaḷ eraḍarasadr̥śate tān āge negaḷda śabdōpāttaṁpadapiṁ pratītam āg’ int’idiroḷ bhēdaman usirvoḍaṁvyatirēkaṁ

The boldface words are identical lexical items. Once the verses are put side-by-side in the same script, anyone can see that the latter is a close adaptation of the former; indeed, a reader of Sanskrit who has no Kannada at all might even guess what the Kannada text says. It is, in other words, an indisputable fact that Kannada texts share a vocabulary, a set of concepts, and a set of intertextual references with other South Asian languages. Romanization is one way of making scholars cognizant of these linkages and inviting them to reflect on them.

Roman transliteration has other applications as well. It is sometimes considered typographically inelegant to introduce radical changes in script shapes within a line of text, and hence publications that are written in European languages often transliterate Indic-language text, even in cases where the readers might be expected to know the script.

Romanization is often desirable for technical reasons, too: although the adoption of Unicode is now nearly universal, and many applications support storing data in non-Roman characters, it is generally more convenient to store and manipulate data in transliterated form. These applications are largely beyond the scope of this paper, in part because there are different requirements for human-readable and machine-readable transliteration systems, but they will be discussed briefly in an appendix.

Strict and loose transliteration

In the context of the transliteration of Indic scripts into Roman characters, we would like to make a distinction between strict and loose transliteration that we will refer to in the following guidelines. Kannada, like many other scripts, provides multiple ways of orthographically representing certain combinations of sounds. In some cases — for instance, when one is transcribing a manuscript or inscription — it is desirable to record all of the orthographic distinctions made in the source document, even if they are not linguistically significant. In other cases, it is not necessary to record these distinctions. That is, in strict transliteration, the transliterator will follow the representational choices of the source document as closely as possible, while in loose transliteration, the transliterator will normalize these choices to a greater or lesser extent. Some contexts where a document written in the Kannada script will write the “same” sounds in different ways are (a) word-initial vowels, (b) word-final consonants, and (c) nasals preceding consonants. These cases are to be distinguished from cases of orthographic variation strictly speaking, where different characters are employed to represent “the same” sounds, which will be discussed below.

In the case of word-initial vowels, in scriptio continua, the vowel is generally written with a dependent vowel sign that is attached to a preceding consonant, if there is one. Otherwise, the vowel is written with an independent sign. These two modes of representing the vowel are generally considered to be equivalent, and they are generally disregarded in transliteration. This is a feature of what we call loose transliteration. In some cases, as noted above, it is desirable to distinguish between independent and dependent vowel signs. A strict system of transliteration distinguishes between dependent and independent vowels.

In the case of final consonants, they too may be written in two ways, if the following word begins with a consonant. Either the two adjoining consonants are written as a conjunct, or the first consonant is written with a marker, called virāma, to cancel its inherent vowel—resembling a squiggle at the top line of the consonant (್)—and the second consonant is written as a separate grapheme. Once again, in loose transliteration the distinction between these two ways of writing adjoining consonants is ignored, whereas in strict transliteration, the distinction is preserved.

Typically a nasal before another consonant is written with the anusvāra marker rather than the nasal consonant character. In loose transliteration, the choice of anusvāra or the place nasal is considered a matter of orthography, and normalized. In strict transliteration, the source document’s use of either anusvāra or the corresponding place nasal (parasavarṇaṁ) is followed.

Finally, a strictly transliterated document would generally avoid inserting spaces between words, since most premodern Kannada documents, as noted above, do not use interword spacing. Some inscriptions and manuscripts do employ interword spacing, however, and in those cases, we recommend the use of the sign . Nevertheless spaces may be used in strict transliteration if it is made clear that they represent the editor’s or transliterator’s additions.

Transliteration schemes past and present

When we turn to how scholars have, in practice, dealt with the problem of representing Kannada text — that is to say, the Kannada language, written in the Kannada script — in Roman transliteration, we find that several transliteration schemes have been used. All of these transliteration schemes were historically designed to represent the Sanskrit language, written in the Dēvanāgarī script, in the Latin alphabet. Thus publications such as Epigraphia Indica or Indian Antiquary which included transliterated texts in multiple South Asian languages typically used the same signs to represent the same sounds in different languages. Hence, in Indian Antiquary, a long a-vowel was written as â, and a retroflex sibilant as sh, whether the text was written in Sanskrit or Kannada, or any other language. Today, the favored transliteration for the signs representing these sounds is ā and ṣ respectively. These “shared” transliteration systems have been “extended” with various diacritics and characters in order to accommodate other languages and scripts. In this connection, we must mention that the Kannada language, and hence Kannada writing systems, make several distinctions that are not made in Sanskrit.

  • 11Śabdamaṇidarpaṇaṁ (Jewel-Mirror of Language) 8: savanisid’ e ē o ō emb’ ivu kannaḍadoḷ.

The first is the distinction between short and long versions of the mid vowels. In Sanskrit, the vowel phonemes eː and oː are inherently long. There has hence been no need to differentiate them from the corresponding short vowels. Thus the vowels eː and oː — written as ए and ओ in the Dēvanāgarī script — are written simply as e and o in all of the most common systems of transliteration for Sanskrit (the International Alphabet for Sanskrit Transliteration [IAST] and ISO-15919). Kannada, however, distinguishes between short e and long eː (written ಎ and ಏ respectively), as well as between short o and long oː (written ಒ and ಓ respectively).11 This distinction is not made consistently in palm-leaf manuscripts. The sign for short e and o is the default sign for both sounds: that is, in our experience, one will often see a long eː written with the sign for a short e, but very rarely the reverse.

  • 12Śabdamaṇidarpaṇaṁ 18.

The second is the presence of several additional liquids. Besides r (ರ), Kannada has a consonant which is written as ಱ, and nowadays called the “cart r” (śakaṭa-rēpha) after its shape. Although a distinction is not now made in pronunciation, it is related to the alveolar trill still found in Tamil. Kannada also has two lateral sounds besides l (ಲ), which it shares with Sanskrit. The first is a retroflex lateral, ɭ, which is written as ಳ. In premodern grammars, the sound is called kṣaḷa when it occurs in Sanskrit and Sanskrit-derived words, being identical with a sound that is found in some pronunciations of Sanskrit and written as ळ in Dēvanāgarī. When it occurs in Kannada words, it is called kuḷa.12 Finally, there is a retroflex approximant ɻ which is written as ೞ. This sound, called raḷa, has largely merged with the kuḷa in modern Kannada. These three consonants, ಱ, ಳ, and ೞ, require additional signs or markers if, as has been the case with all historically deployed transliteration schemes for Kannada, a system designed for Sanskrit is taken as a starting point.

One of the earliest transliteration systems was employed by Ferdinand Kittel, first in his Canarese Prosody (1875) and then in his Kannada–English Dictionary (first ed. 1894). It is an extension of a system of transliteration that was then used for Sanskrit, which differs from most modern systems in quite a few respects. It is Kittel’s treatment of the distinctions specific to Kannada, however, that interests us here. He wrote short e and o with a breve to indicate that they were short, thus ĕ = ಎ and ŏ = ಒ. In the interest of a complete lack of ambiguity, however, he also wrote the corresponding long vowels, eː and oː, with macrons, thus ē = ಏ and ō = ಓ. This system of “double-marking” was not widely followed: Kittel’s successors generally chose to mark either short vowels with a breve, or long vowels with a macron, but not both.

For the three sounds ಱ, ಳ and ೞ Kittel adopted the transcriptions r̤, ḷ, and l̤. Double dots were used for r̤ and l̤ because a single dot below the consonant was already in use for the vocalic r, which he transcribed as ṛ, and the retroflex l. Unlike his “double-marking” of the e- and o-vowels, Kittel’s use of r̤ and l̤ for ಱ and ೞ was used in other publications, such as Epigraphia Indica and Indian Antiquary.

  • 13 One recent work of scholarship which employs Kittel’s transliteration system is Pollock (2006).

While Kittel’s transliteration choices are still sometimes followed today, they have largely been replaced by a system wherein ಱ, ಳ and ೞ are represented by ṟ, ḷ, and ḻ.13 The ISO-15919 guidelines present such a system. This system has the benefit, on the one hand, of avoiding ambiguity with other values in other Indic scripts (in ISO-15919, ṛ represents a retroflex flap, and r̥ represents a vocalic r, while l̥ represents a vocalic l) and, on the other, of aligning with the currently-favored transliteration of the corresponding sounds in other South Indian languages, such as Tamil. Today, ṟ and ḻ are usually used to transliterate the Tamil characters ற் and ழ், although r̤ and l̤ were used in earlier publications such as Epigraphia Indica.

The major difference in modern transliteration systems is the treatment of the mid vowels. In one, the short vowels are the “default” vowels, and do not receive any diacritical marks (hence e and o), whereas the long vowels receive a macron (hence ē and ō), and in the other, the long vowels are the “default” (hence e and o), whereas the short vowels receive a breve (hence ĕ and ŏ). The first has the advantage of treating all of the vowels in the same fashion, that is, by transcribing the short vowel without diacritics, and the long vowel with a macron. It has the disadvantage of differing from the most commonly employed systems of transliterating Sanskrit, such as IAST. In the second system, the situation is exactly the reverse: conformity with Sanskrit is achieved, at the cost of introducing an entirely new diacritical mark.

There are two features of our transliteration system that call for comment. The first is the manner in which the distinction between the short vowels e and o and the long vowels eː and oː is represented. We have chosen to represent the short vowels without any diacritical marks, and to use macrons over the long vowels. As noted above, this means that Sanskrit words containing these mid vowels will be transcribed with a macron: bōdhi, vēda, etc. We realize that this departs from the practice of IAST (bodhi, veda) and that to many Sanskrit scholars it may look strange. The ISO-15919 standard allows the mid vowels of Bengali, Devanagari, Gurmukhi, Gujarati and Oriya to be transliterated with e and o, without macrons, and this appears to be the most common application of ISO-15919 for Sanskrit (based, evidently, upon the writing of Sanskrit in the Devanagari script). Nevertheless, we feel that a clear mark of the length of the vowel is useful even in Sanskrit, where ē and ō always have the same duration as ā, ī, and ū. Moreover the “uniform vowels” option of the ISO-15919, which is required when the scripts involved include separate characters for long and short e and o, requires that the mid vowels of the aforementioned scripts be transliterated as ē and ō. The use of the brevis for the short mid vowels would serve the sole purpose of reserving e and o for long vowels. If the “uniform vowels” option of the ISO-15919 standard is used, there is no reason to use a brevis as a diacritical marker. Scholars accustomed to IAST may also balk at the use of r̥ and l̥, but they are already a part of the ISO-15919 standard, and they are essential for avoiding ambiguity with ṛ and ḷ: the former, representing a retroflex flap, is not used in Kannada, but it is in many other South Asian languages, and the latter, representing the retroflex lateral approximant, is in fact used in Kannada.

The other feature of this system is the optional use of the marker ˚ to indicate that the vowel has been written with an independent vowel sign. This sign has been adopted, by way of Griffiths et al. 2017, from Louis-Charles Damais (Griffiths et al. 2017: 73). The ISO-15919 standard does not dedicate a character to indicating independent vowels, but recommends the use of the colon (:) to indicate a hiatus. The marker ˚ will generally be used in a strict transliteration. Hence ಜನೊಕ್ತಿಇಂದಂ — a form found in a manuscript that would typically be normalized in a loose transliteration to janōktiyindaṁ — would be rendered janokti˚iṁdaṁ in a strict transliteration.

Orthographic considerations

When producing a strict transliteration, the goal is generally to represent the source document as closely as possible, character-by-character. In many other use cases, however, scholars will want to present a text that follows a standard orthography. In the following section, we will outline an orthography of word separation. In this section, we make one additional orthographic recommendation: the use of the class nasal (parasavarṇaṁ) instead of anusvāra within a word.

In manuscript, inscriptions, and most printed editions, a preconsonantal nasal within a word (e.g. saṅgaṁ, sañcayaṁ, ṣaṇḍaṁ, santaṁ, or sampadaṁ) is usually written with anusvāra (hence ಸಂಗಂ, ಸಂಚಯಂ, ಷಂಡಂ, ಸಂತಂ, and ಸಂಪದಂ for these examples). However, these sounds are sometimes also written with the class nasal (hence ಸಙ್ಗಂ, ಸಞ್ಚಯಂ, ಷಣ್ಡಂ, ಸನ್ತಂ, and ಸಮ್ಪದಂ). Some styles of transliteration follow the use of anusvāra and write, e.g., saṁgaṁ, saṁcayaṁ, ṣaṁḍaṁ, saṁtaṁ, and saṁpadaṁ. It is our preference to use the class nasal (parasavarṇaṁ) in transliteration when the nasal occurs before a consonant and in the middle of a word. (Anusvāra is used in this context when there is no corresponding class nasal, for instance before sibilant consonants.) When an anusvāra represents a word-final nasal, which also happens to be a syllable-final nasal, we maintain the anusvāra. Hence:

  1. ಅಂತು → antu
  2. ಶಂಕರಂ → śaṅkaraṁ
  3. ಬೆಡಂಗುಂ → beḍaṅguṁ
  4. ಮರಂಗಳ್ → maraṅgaḷ
  5. ಒಡಂಬಡು → oḍambaḍu
  6. ಶಬ್ದದಿಂದೆ → śabdadinde
  7. ಸಂಶಯಂ → saṁśayaṁ

But:

  1. ನೃಪಂ ನೆಗೞೆ → nr̥paṁ negaḻe

These considerations, of course, do not apply to strict transliteration.

There were other kinds of orthographic variation in premodern Kannada, including several phenomena that may represent variation in pronunciation as well. For example, manuscripts and editions will write either ಸೈತು (saitu) or ಸಯ್ತು (saytu), or ಕೈ (kai) or ಕೆಯ್ (key). In this document, we do not take a position on the “correct” way to write these variants. Rather, we believe that the orthography of a text edition should, other things being equal, conform to the orthography of its sources, whether these are manuscripts or inscriptions. Hence these decisions should be left to editors.

4 Word Separation

The following guidelines for word separation are based on the principle that a reader who requires or prefers a text in romanized form will also require or prefer the boundaries between words — which, as noted above, are generally not marked in Kannada — to be indicated as well. Once again, these considerations do not apply to strict transliteration. Rather, they are guidelines for a consistent orthography of romanized Kannada text.

In proposing the following approach to word-separation in romanized Kannada text, we assume that the analyzed text should be phonemically identical to the unanalyzed text. By “phonemically identical” we mean that there should be no phonemes — for these purposes, vowel or consonant sounds — that are represented in the analyzed text and not in the unanalyzed text and vice versa. This constraint means that the analyzed text should “read” like the original, unanalyzed text, with the same metrical structure. Accordingly it excludes certain methods of analysis, including the restoration of final vowels that have been lost due to sandhi (see above), which are undoubtedly useful for pedagogical purposes. Rendering the sequence ಅಂತಾಗಿantāgi, for example, as antu āgi, makes it clear that the final vowel u of the first word antu has been lost in lōpasandhi, but at the cost of introducing an additional syllable. What we are aiming for in these guidelines, by contrast, is a way of indicating word-breaks that largely maintains the sandhi and hence the metrical structure of the original unanalyzed text.

In fact, in cases where the final consonant of one word forms a single syllable with the initial vowel of the following word, the use of the Roman script will easily allow us to indicate word boundaries with spaces, while the use of the Kannada script for the same text will not. Those who are transliterating a text into Roman letters will, in such cases, be inclined to insert spaces between words without thinking about it. For example:

  1. ಮರಮಕ್ಕುಂ → maram akkuṁ
  2. ಮನದೊಳೊಂದಿ → manadoḷ ondi

For other scenarios, however, we must resort to other devices in order to indicate a word boundary. Some of those scenarios are discussed below.

Elision (lōpasandhi)

We recommend the use of a final apostrophe to represent a vowel that has been elided by lōpasandhi, followed by a space indicating the word boundary. The vowel that has disappeared should generally not be restored, although if it is necessary for purposes of disambiguation, the vowel may be placed in square brackets:

  1. ಎನಗೀಗೆ → enag’ īge (→ enag[e] īge)
  2. ಮನದಿಂದೊಂದಿ → manadind’ ondi (→ manadind[e] ondi)
  3. ಅಂತಾಗಿ → ant’ āgi (→ ant[u] āgi)

This practice is similar to the use of an apostrophe to mark the elision of an initial vowel in Sanskrit (tatō ’pi = tataḥ + api). The difference is that the apostrophe is a transcriptional equivalent to the avagraha sign in Sanskrit, whereas in the case of Kannada lōpasandhi, the apostrophe does not correspond to anything in the Kannada-script text.

For lexicalized phrases, such as ಒಂದೊಂದು ondondu, the apostrophe and space should not be introduced, since there is no word boundary.

Facultative consonants (āgamasandhi)

When one word ends in a vowel, and the following word begins with a vowel, a consonant is often inserted between them, as noted above. Our recommendation is to write the consonant at the end of the first wordEven in the cases where the inserted consonant is homorganic with that of the second word (e.g. in Tamil ...u t-tiru...) [but this might not be in fact āgamasandhi proper?], before the space that separates it from the following word. The word-final consonant will not be confused with a case-ending.

In the following cases, the consonant is inserted according to a phonological rule of glide insertion: -y- is inserted after i, ī, e, and ē, and -v- after a, ā, u, ū, o, and ō.

  1. ಸಮವೃತ್ತಿಯಪ್ಪವೋಲ್ → samavr̥ttiy appavōl
  2. ಎಣೆಯಕ್ಕರಂ → eṇey akkaraṁ
  3. ಪಲವೆಡೆಗಳೊಳ್ → palav eḍegaḷoḷ

Note that this differs from the practice of Epigraphia Indica, which was to write the glide at the beginning of the second word (e.g., samavr̥tti yappavōl).

In a number of other cases, however, what is today called āgamasandhi falls under a completely different description, according to which a word-final consonant that is neither pronounced nor written when the word occurs on its own “resurfaces” when followed by a vowel. The disappearance of word-final consonants in pronunciation and writing is a feature of the modern language. Here, too, the consonant should be written at the end of the word to which it morphologically belongs. Thus the word ಮನುಷ್ಯಂmanuṣyaṁ ends with a consonant, namely a nasal, in premodern Kannada, but in modern Kannada, it is pronounced and written as ಮನುಷ್ಯmanuṣya. But when it is followed by the enclitic particle ಏ (ē), it is pronounced and written as:

  1. ಮನುಷ್ಯನೇ = manuṣyan ē

One more special case might be mentioned. A general sound change in Kannada turned a word-final ṁ into v when it was followed by a word beginning with a vowel. This -v appears very commonly before the function-words āgi and āda. A sequence such as ಸ್ಪಷ್ಟವಾಗಿspaṣṭavāgi (“clearly”) would probably be thought of as a single expression, and we would therefore recommend the spaceless form spaṣṭavāgi. But if additional clarity is desired, the separated form spaṣṭav āgi may be used, as per the transliterator’s discretion.

Enclitic particles

Kannada has a number of enclitic particles which pose a challenge to word-separation, insofar as they form a single phonological word with the word that they follow (their “host”), but nevertheless should not necessarily be seen as modifying their host in the manner of an inflectional ending. Whereas suffixes modify the meaning of the individual lexical element to which they are added, particles can modify the meaning of the sentence as a whole, although they can also modify sub-sentential constituents such as clauses and words. In at least one case, it would be impossible to separate a particle from its host without also separating its host word from an inflection. When the additive particle -uṁ is added to a word with the accusative ending -aṁ, the ending is -umaṁ rather than -amuṁ. We do not recommend separating the additive particle from its host word with a space, dash, or any other contrivance.

Similarly, the exclusive particle -e should not be separated from its host word. It is sometimes difficult to tell when the exclusive particle actually excludes something and when it is merely part of an inflectional suffix (e.g., -inde, -ine, -oḷe, -oḷage, etc.). The additive and exclusive particles should thus be considered “quasi-endings” and written together with their host words.

The exclamatory and interrogative particles ē, o, ō, and ā are similarly to be written together with the previous word, although for clarity they can optionally be separated from their host with a dash.

  1. ದೊರೆಯೇ → doreyē or dorey-ē
  2. ಯಶೋಭಾಗಿಯೋ → yaśōbhāgiyō or yaśōbhāgiy-ō
  3. ಮಗನೇ → maganē or magan-ē
  4. ಪಂಪೊ → pempo or pemp-o
  5. ಇಲ್ಲೆಯೋ → illeyō or illey-ō
  6. ಕರಮೆ → karame or karam-e
  7. ಒಂದೆ → onde or ond-e

Kannada compounds

Another area where different styles of orthography for romanized Kannada text have arisen is that of compound words, both in Kannada and in Sanskrit. Scholars have sometimes chosen to represent a boundary between the constituent words of a compound, and sometimes chosen to leave such boundaries unmarked. Since one of the main purposes of romanizing Kannada text is to make it accessible to people who may not be familiar with the way the Kannada language works, we prefer the former option. Hence in the orthography we favor for romanized Kannada, the boundaries between words in a compound will be marked with a dash (-). As we will see, however, this is generally only possible when the boundary between constituents of a compound coincides with the boundary between phonemes. This is usually the case in Kannada, and less usually the case in Sanskrit. We may note, once again, that the marking of word boundaries is a matter of the orthography of romanized Kannada, and finds no scope in strict transliteration.

This is not the place to enter into a discussion of which collocations of words in Kannada constitute “compounds.” One diagnostic feature, however, of Kannada compounds is the lenition (weakening) of the initial consonant of the following word.   2. ಬಾಯೞಿ → bāy-aḻi “to lose one’s voice”
  3. ಜಾಣ್ಗಿಡೆ → jāṇ-giḍe (kiḍe) “cleverness-wasting”
  4. ಪೞಗನ್ನದಂ → paḻa-gannaḍaṁ (kannaḍaṁ) “old Kannada”
  5. ಒಳ್ಗರುಳ್ → oḷ-garuḷ (karuḷ) “inner intestines”

  9. ತೆಂಬೆಲರ್ → tem-belar “south wind”
  10. ಆಱಡಿ → āṟ-aḍi “six-foot” (bee)
  11. ಈರೊಡಲ್ → īr-oḍal “two bodies”

Many compounds are lexicalized, and in some cases the introduction of a dash between members of a compound might seem like an unnecessary intervention. Nevertheless we prefer to maintain the separation even in such cases. Separating compounds like this will make it easier to recognize their parts, and also make it easier to perform computer-assisted lemmatization.

Although compounds made up of numerals do not differ in linguistic terms from those listed above, we nevertheless do not recommend inserting a dash into the seam of these compounds. For the purposes of this orthography, such compounds can be treated as independent lexical items. Nor do we consider derivational suffixes, such as the ordinal suffix -aneya-, to be independent lexical items. Hence:

  1. ಎಂಬತ್ತು → embattu “eighty”
  2. ಇನ್ನೂರು → innūru “two hundred”
  3. ಮೂಱನೆಯ → mūṟaneya “third”

Sanskrit compounds

Many Sanskrit compounds appear in Kannada text. Even with the field of Sanskrit studies, no consensus has emerged regarding how, if at all, to separate the constituents of a Sanskrit compound. The approaches can be classified under three headings.

  • Minimalist approaches do not attempt to mark the boundary between members of a compound word at all. Thus hr̥tkalike, niyamōkti, gajānanaṁ.
  • Maximalist approaches attempt to mark the seam of every compound as a matter of principle. This includes, for example, the analysis of every sandhi combination, both within and between words, where the preceding three words would be represented as hr̥d-kalike, niyama-ukti, and gaja-ānanaṁ. But it also includes the practice of the Clay Sanskrit Library, which developed conventions for representing certain types of sandhi combinations without restoring lost letters. Hence, in the Clay Sanskrit Library style, the preceding words would be rendered as hr̥t|kalike, niyam’’|ôkti, and gaj’|ânanaṁ.
  • Opportunistic approaches insert a separator between constituents if the internal word boundary coincides with a boundary between phonemes, and generally not otherwise. One approach would render the above words hr̥t-kalike, niyamōkti, and gajānanaṁ. One type of “opportunistic” approach, which is more “maximalist” than the one just described, uses special diacritical marks — usually circumflexes — to represent long vowels that are the result of sandhi, hence niyamôkti and gajânanaṁ.

In these guidelines we do not take a stand on which of these approaches is preferable for Sanskrit; they all have their strengths and weaknesses. It appears, however, that the opportunistic approach is most consonant with the orthography for romanized Kannada presented here, in that it does not add any symbols to the repertoire beyond what is already required for representing Kannada, and it does not use symbols such as the apostrophe in ways different from those that have been defined for representing Kannada.

The following compounds can be separated opportunistically:

  1. ರವಿಕರಂ → ravi-karaṁ (ravi- + kara-)
  2. ರನನ್ಮಧುಕರಂ → raṇan-madhukaraṁ (raṇat- + madhukara-)
  3. ಹೃದಾನಂದಂ → hr̥d-ānandaṁ (hr̥t- + ānanda-)
  4. ದ್ವ್ಯಕ್ಷರಂ → dvy-akṣaraṁ (dvi- + akṣara-)
  5. ಶರಚ್ಛ್ರಿ → śarac-chri (śarat- + śrī-)

In the following compounds, however, a word-final vowel and a word-initial vowel have coalesced to produce a single vowel, and there is no convenient way of marking the boundary between constituents without introducing additional symbols:

  1. ಕುವಲಯಾನಂದಂ → kuvalayānandaṁ (kuvalaya- + ānandaṁ)
  2. ಮುನೀಶ್ವರಂ → munīśvaraṁ (muni + īśvaraṁ)
  3. ಛಂದೋನುಶಾಸನಂ → chandōnuśāsanaṁ (chandas- + anuśāsanaṁ)

The use of circumflex accents to mark vowels resulting from sandhi (hence kuvalayânandaṁ, munîśvaraṁ, and perhaps chandônuśāsanaṁ) is not recommended.

5 Metrical Boundaries

In Kannada a boundary between two metrical lines — the end of one line and the beginning of the next — often occurs in the middle of a word. In manuscripts and inscriptions, verses are most commonly written in scriptio continua, with a vertical bar or daṇḍaṁ (| or ||) used to mark boundaries between metrical lines. (In our experience, a single bar marks the boundaries between the second and third lines of a four-line verse, and a double bar marks the end of the verse; the boundaries between the odd and even lines of a verse are not marked at all.) In printed editions, it has become common to typeset each metrical line on a separate typographic line. What do we do when a single word is broken across two typographic lines?

In many editions of texts printed in the Kannada script, there is no indication that a word has been broken across a typographic line boundary. Hence the following verse would be printed just so (Kavirājamārgaṁ 2.3):

  1. ಅನ್ನೆಗಮದಱೊಳಗೆ ಸಮುತ್ಪನ್ನಪ್ರಾಧಾನ್ಯಮನ್ಯಮರ್ಥಾಧಾರಂಮುನ್ನಂ ಶಬ್ದಾಳಂಕಾರಂ ನಿಶ್ಚಿತಮಕ್ಕೆ ಪೇೞ್ವ ಮಾೞ್ಕೆಯೊಳೆನ್ನಾ

According to our guidelines, this would be transliterated as follows:

  1. annegam adaṟoḷage samutpanna-prādhānyam anyam arthādhāraṁmunnaṁ śabdāḷaṅkāraṁ niścitam akke pēḻva māḻkeyoḷ ennā

The word samutpanna straddles the first and second lines, and similarly the word alaṅkāra straddles the the third and fourth lines. Typically the breaking of a word across a typographic line would be indicated with a dash. But the dash is reserved in our orthography for indicating the juncture between constituents of a compounds. Our provisional recommendation is to use the tilde to mark such breaks if and only if they do not coincide with the break between members of a compound. Hence:

  1. annegam adaṟoḷage samut~panna-prādhānyam anyam arthādhāraṁmunnaṁ śabdāḷaṅkā~raṁ niścitam akke pēḻva māḻkeyoḷ ennā

(For samut~ // panna-, see below.) But the following verse, wherein the line-breaks coincide with breaks within a compound word, would be represented differently:

  1. ಪರಿವಾರವಾರವನಿತಾಪರಿವೃತನರಮನೆಯೊಳರಸನಂಬರತಳದೊಳ್ವರ ತಾರಾಪರಿಗತ ಶಶಧರನಂತೊಪ್ಪಿರೆ ವಿನೋದದಿಂ ಜನಿತಮುದಂ
  2. parivāra-vāra-vanitā-parivr̥tan ara-maneyoḷ arasan ambarataḷadoḷvara-tārā-parigata-śaśa-dharan ant’ oppire vinōdadiṁ janitamudaṁ

Because the word-boundary within a compound is already marked with a dash, there is no need to add an additional indication of a break with a tilde.

When a word is broken across lines in Kannada script, the question of which letters go before the line-break, and which letters go afterwards, is easily answered by the syllabic logic of the script. In romanized Kannada, however, we can not only break between syllables, but also between phonemes. The two words here are manadoḷ and ondu. There is no reason, apart from following the syllabic logic of the Kannada script, to write manado- // ḷ ondu.

In the case of samut- // panna, we have elected to break the line according to morpheme boundaries rather than preserve the integrity of the Kannada character tpa. The representation samu~ // tpanna would also be possible. Some judgment is required for such cases. By contrast, when there is a final anusvāra — whether it is represented in transliteration by an anusvāra or a class nasal — it is recommended to put the nasal before the line-break. Hence en- // duṁ, and never e~ // nduṁ.

6 Conclusion and Samples

The recommendations in this paper apply only to Kannada text that is presented in romanized form. There are, however, a variety of use cases for romanized Kannada text. When producing a transcription of a manuscript or inscription, it will generally be desirable to represent the source document as closely as possible, using what we have called “strict transliteration” in this document. For most other purposes, it will be desirable to represent the text according to a standard orthography. In both cases there is need of a standard system of transliteration. We have argued for using the ISO-15919 system with the “uniform vowels” option to meet this need, and we have specified a number of additional conventions for representing features of Kannada manuscripts and inscriptions in “strict transliteration” that are not captured by the ISO-15919 guidelines. In addition, for the purposes of presenting an orthographically normalized text that represents word boundaries to the degree possible, we have outlined a standard orthography for romanized Kannada text. Our recommendations concern the addition of spaces between words, the use of apostrophes to mark the elision of final vowels, and the use of dashes to mark the seams of compound words. Finally, we offer some suggestions for the problem of breaking words over metrical and typographic lines.
